# 4.1 SLOs, SLAs & Error Budgets

## Why Reliability Needs Numbers

Every engineering team says their system is "highly available" and "reliable." These are meaningless without numbers.

"Highly available" could mean 99% uptime (87 hours of downtime per year) or 99.99% (52 minutes per year). The difference is enormous — both in engineering cost and in what customers can actually build on top of your platform.

SLOs, SLAs, and error budgets turn "reliable" into a precise, measurable, and actionable engineering concept. Without them, reliability work is driven by whoever shouts loudest after an incident. With them, reliability is managed like a budget — spent deliberately, tracked continuously, and defended with data.

---

## Definitions — Getting the Vocabulary Right

### SLI — Service Level Indicator

An SLI is a quantitative measurement of a specific aspect of service behavior. It is a number you can measure.

Examples of SLIs for Pravah:

```
Job dispatch latency:       time between pipeline trigger and runner receiving the job
Job success rate:           percentage of jobs that complete without error
API request latency:        p99 response time for POST /pipelines
Runner assignment rate:     percentage of jobs assigned to a runner within 10s
Kafka consumer lag:         seconds behind real-time for job.events consumer
Pipeline trigger accuracy:  percentage of cron-triggered pipelines that fire within 60s of scheduled time
```

An SLI must be measurable in real-time, reflect user experience, and be meaningful to the business — not just technically interesting.

**Bad SLI:** "CPU utilization of execution-service pods" — nobody's pipeline failed because CPU was at 80%.

**Good SLI:** "Percentage of jobs that start executing within 30 seconds of being triggered" — this is exactly what users care about.

### SLO — Service Level Objective

An SLO is the target value (or range) for an SLI over a rolling time window.

```
SLI:    Job success rate (percentage of jobs completing successfully)
SLO:    ≥ 99.5% of jobs succeed, measured over a rolling 28-day window

SLI:    API p99 latency for POST /pipelines
SLO:    p99 < 500ms, measured over a rolling 7-day window

SLI:    Pipeline trigger accuracy
SLO:    ≥ 99.9% of scheduled triggers fire within 60 seconds, rolling 28-day window

SLI:    Runner assignment time
SLO:    ≥ 99% of jobs assigned to a runner within 30 seconds, rolling 28-day window
```

SLOs are internal commitments. They are what your engineering team holds itself to. They should be set *below* what is technically achievable — you need headroom to deploy, experiment, and handle unexpected load.

### SLA — Service Level Agreement

An SLA is a contractual commitment to a customer, with financial consequences if breached (credits, refunds, right to terminate).

```
SLA for Pravah Enterprise:
  - Platform availability: 99.9% monthly uptime
  - Job execution: 99% of jobs complete within 2× their historical average duration
  - Breach penalty: 10% service credit for each 0.1% below 99.9% availability
```

**The critical relationship:** SLO must be stricter than SLA. Your SLO is your internal warning system — you must be alerted and fix things before the SLA is breached.

```
SLA (external): 99.9% availability
SLO (internal): 99.95% availability ← you must fix things here
                ↑
         50% margin before SLA breach
```

If your SLO and SLA are the same number, you find out about SLA breaches at the same time as your customers. That is too late.

## Error Budgets — The Core Concept

An error budget is the amount of unreliability you are allowed in a given period, derived from your SLO.

```
SLO:          99.9% success rate over 28 days
Total events: 10,000,000 job executions over 28 days
Allowed failures: 10,000,000 × (1 - 0.999) = 10,000 jobs

Error budget: 10,000 failed jobs per 28-day period
```

If you have consumed 8,000 failures with 10 days left in the window: you have 2,000 failures remaining across 10 days. This is data, not intuition.

### How Error Budgets Change Engineering Behavior

**Without error budgets:**

- Feature teams push fast — they don't know or don't care about reliability cost
- On-call engineers say "we can't deploy this week" without data to back it up
- Post-incident: arguments about whose fault it was, no systematic improvement
- Reliability work competes with feature work with no framework to arbitrate

**With error budgets:**

- Shared budget creates shared responsibility between feature teams and platform
- When budget is healthy (plenty remaining): move fast, ship features, run experiments
- When budget is nearly exhausted: freeze risky deployments, prioritize reliability work
- Decisions are data-driven, not political

**The error budget policy defines what happens at each threshold:**

```
Budget consumed    Policy
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
< 50%              Normal operations. Deployments proceed.
                   Experiments allowed. Features ship.

50–75%             Elevated caution. Deployments require
                   review. No risky migrations or schema changes.
                   Reliability work gets sprint priority.

75–99%             Deployment freeze for non-critical changes.
                   All engineering focus on reliability.
                   On-call rotations intensified.

> 99% (exhausted)  Full deployment freeze.
                   Emergency reliability sprint.
                   Executive notification.
                   Post-mortem required before resuming.
```

This policy is agreed upon by engineering, product, and leadership before it is needed — not negotiated during an incident.

## Pravah's SLO Hierarchy

Not all components have the same reliability requirements. A well-designed SLO hierarchy reflects the different guarantees different parts of Pravah need to make.

### Tier 1 — Critical Path (highest reliability)

These directly impact whether customer jobs run.

```
SLO-1: Job Execution Success Rate
  SLI:    Percentage of triggered jobs that complete successfully
  Target: 99.5% over rolling 28 days
  Rationale: 0.5% failure rate (50,000 failures per 10M jobs) accounts for
             user errors, transient runner failures, and infrastructure blips.
             99.9% would be too strict — some failures are expected and correct
             (user's code is wrong, resource limits hit).

SLO-2: Job Dispatch Latency
  SLI:    p99 time from pipeline trigger to job received by runner
  Target: p99 < 10 seconds over rolling 7 days
  Rationale: A job queued for >10s is user-visible. Sub-second is ideal
             but pipeline trigger → Kafka → Execution → assignment → gRPC
             has irreducible latency at p99 under load.

SLO-3: Pipeline Trigger Accuracy
  SLI:    Percentage of cron-scheduled triggers that fire within 60s of scheduled time
  Target: 99.9% over rolling 28 days
  Rationale: Enterprise customers schedule jobs at specific times for
             dependencies. Missing a trigger window causes downstream failures.

SLO-4: Runner Assignment Rate
  SLI:    Percentage of jobs that receive a runner assignment within 30s
  Target: 99% over rolling 28 days
  Rationale: Unassigned jobs for >30s indicate runner starvation or
             assignment service failure. 1% miss accommodates bursts.
```

### Tier 2 — User Experience (high reliability)

These impact how users interact with the platform but do not directly cause job failures.

```
SLO-5: API Availability
  SLI:    Percentage of API requests returning non-5xx response
  Target: 99.9% over rolling 28 days (43 minutes downtime/month)
  Scope:  All API Gateway endpoints

SLO-6: API Latency
  SLI:    p99 response time for GET /pipelines, GET /jobs
  Target: p99 < 500ms over rolling 7 days

SLO-7: Dashboard Freshness
  SLI:    Age of job status data shown on dashboard (replication lag)
  Target: 99% of dashboard reads reflect state within 5 seconds
  Rationale: AP system — slight staleness acceptable, but >5s is noticeable
```

### Tier 3 — Internal Reliability (moderate reliability)

These are internal systems that indirectly support the above.

```
SLO-8: Kafka Consumer Lag
  SLI:    Consumer group lag for job.events in seconds behind real-time
  Target: p95 lag < 5 seconds over rolling 7 days

SLO-9: Outbox Relay Latency
  SLI:    Time from outbox message creation to Kafka publish
  Target: p99 < 2 seconds over rolling 7 days

SLO-10: Metadata Search Latency
  SLI:    p99 response time for Elasticsearch catalog queries
  Target: p99 < 1 second over rolling 7 days
```

## Measuring SLIs in Pravah

SLIs must be measured from the user's perspective, not the system's internal perspective. This is a subtle but critical distinction.

**Wrong approach — measuring from inside the system:**
```
Job success rate = (jobs marked COMPLETE in database) / (total jobs created)
```
This misses: jobs that were never created due to an API error, jobs stuck in PENDING due to a bug, jobs the runner silently dropped.

**Right approach — measuring from the user's boundary:**
```
Job success rate = (jobs that returned SUCCESS to the triggering API call or webhook)
                 / (jobs that were accepted by the API with 2xx response)
```

This is measured at the API Gateway boundary — the point where Pravah's responsibility begins.

### Prometheus Recording Rules for Pravah's SLOs

```yaml
# Prometheus recording rules — pre-compute SLI values
# Evaluated every 30 seconds, stored as time series

groups:
  - name: pravah_slo_rules
    interval: 30s
    rules:

      # SLO-1: Job success rate (5-minute window, later aggregated to 28-day)
      - record: pravah:job_success_rate:5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(pravah_job_completed_total{status="SUCCESS"}[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(pravah_job_completed_total[5m]))

      # SLO-2: Job dispatch p99 latency
      - record: pravah:job_dispatch_latency_p99:5m
        expr: |
          histogram_quantile(0.99,
            sum(rate(pravah_job_dispatch_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])) by (le)
          )

      # SLO-5: API error rate (inverse of availability)
      - record: pravah:api_error_rate:5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{
            status=~"5..",
            application="api-gateway"
          }[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{
            application="api-gateway"
          }[5m]))

      # SLO-6: API p99 latency
      - record: pravah:api_latency_p99:5m
        expr: |
          histogram_quantile(0.99,
            sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_bucket{
              application="api-gateway",
              uri=~"/pipelines.*|/jobs.*"
            }[5m])) by (le, uri)
          )
```

### Rolling 28-Day Error Budget Burn Rate

Burning the error budget slowly is normal. Burning it fast is an emergency. The burn rate tells you how urgently you need to act.

```yaml
      # Error budget burn rate — how fast are we consuming the budget?
      # 1x burn rate = exactly on track to exhaust budget in 28 days
      # 2x burn rate = budget will be exhausted in 14 days
      # 14x burn rate = budget will be exhausted in 2 days → page on-call now

      - record: pravah:job_success_error_budget_burn_rate:1h
        expr: |
          (1 - pravah:job_success_rate:5m) / (1 - 0.995)
          # 0.995 = SLO target (99.5%)
          # result > 1 means burning faster than allowed
          # result > 14 means exhausting budget within 2 days
```

## The Multi-Window, Multi-Burn-Rate Alert

A simple threshold alert ("error rate > 1% for 5 minutes") is noisy and misses slow burns. Google's SRE book introduced the multi-window approach:

```
Two conditions must both be true to page:
  1. Fast burn: error budget burning > 14x in the last 1 hour
     (exhausts budget in 2 days — urgent)
  2. Slow window confirms it's real: error budget burning > 14x in the last 5 minutes
     (not just a momentary spike)

OR:

  1. Medium burn: error budget burning > 6x in the last 6 hours
     (exhausts budget in ~4.5 days — serious)
  2. Confirmed: > 6x in the last 30 minutes
```

This pages on-call for real problems (sustained burns) while ignoring spikes (brief transient failures that don't materially impact the budget).

```yaml
# Alerting rules
groups:
  - name: pravah_slo_alerts
    rules:

      # Critical: fast burn — page immediately
      - alert: PravahJobSuccessRateFastBurn
        expr: |
          pravah:job_success_error_budget_burn_rate:1h > 14
          AND
          pravah:job_success_error_budget_burn_rate:5m > 14
        for: 2m
        labels:
          severity: critical
          team: platform
        annotations:
          summary: "Job success SLO burning at {{ $value }}x rate"
          description: |
            Error budget will be exhausted in {{ div 28 $value | int }} days
            at current burn rate. Immediate action required.
          runbook: "https://wiki.pravah.io/runbooks/job-success-slo-burn"

      # Warning: moderate burn — ticket + monitor
      - alert: PravahJobSuccessRateSlowBurn
        expr: |
          pravah:job_success_error_budget_burn_rate:6h > 6
          AND
          pravah:job_success_error_budget_burn_rate:30m > 6
        for: 15m
        labels:
          severity: warning
          team: platform
        annotations:
          summary: "Job success SLO burning at {{ $value }}x rate"
          description: "Error budget at risk. Investigate within 4 hours."
```

## Error Budget Reviews — Making Them Useful

Error budget reviews happen on a fixed cadence (weekly for Tier 1 SLOs, monthly otherwise). The review answers three questions:

**1. How much budget was consumed this period?**

```
SLO-1 (Job Success Rate):
  Budget available (28 days):  50,000 failures
  Budget consumed:             12,400 failures (24.8%)
  Budget remaining:            37,600 failures
  Trend: Normal — under 1x burn rate. Healthy.

SLO-2 (Job Dispatch Latency p99):
  Budget available (7 days):   0.1% of time windows exceeding 10s = 100 minutes
  Budget consumed:             43 minutes (43%)
  Budget remaining:            57 minutes
  Trend: Elevated — two incidents on Tuesday caused brief spikes.
         Root cause identified (Kafka partition rebalancing). Fix deployed.
```

**2. What caused consumption?**

Map every budget-consuming incident to a category:

```
Category                   Budget Consumed    Action
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Pravah infrastructure bug  8,200 failures     Post-mortem, fix in sprint
Runner user error          2,400 failures     Improve error messages, no action
External dependency        1,800 failures     Added circuit breaker
Unknown / under investigation  0             —
```

**3. Is the SLO correctly calibrated?**

SLOs should be reviewed quarterly:
- If budget is consistently nearly exhausted → SLO may be too strict, or reliability work needed
- If budget is consistently at <5% consumed → SLO may be too loose, could be tightened
- Tightening an SLO raises the quality bar; loosening admits that the current target was unrealistic

## The Reliability vs Velocity Trade-off

Error budgets make explicit the trade-off that always existed implicitly.

Every deployment risks consuming error budget. Every feature experiment risks consuming error budget. Every schema migration risks consuming error budget.

When budget is plentiful: the team has a data-backed argument for moving fast. Engineering management cannot slow things down without data showing why.

When budget is nearly exhausted: the team has a data-backed argument for slowing down. Product management cannot push features without data showing the reliability risk is acceptable.

This is why error budgets are a sociotechnical tool, not just a technical one. They create a shared language between engineering and product that doesn't reduce to "engineering is too slow" vs "product is too demanding."

## FAANG Interview Angles

**"How does Pravah define and measure reliability?"**

> Pravah uses SLOs with multi-window error budgets for each tier of service. The most critical SLO is job execution success rate — 99.5% of triggered jobs must complete successfully over a rolling 28-day window. This gives an error budget of 50,000 failures per 10 million jobs per period.
>
> SLIs are measured at the user boundary — the API Gateway — not from internal metrics. A job counts as a success only if it was accepted by the API and returned a successful result to the caller. Jobs stuck in database limbo due to a bug don't inflate the success number.
>
> We compute SLI values as Prometheus recording rules every 30 seconds and track the error budget burn rate. A 14x burn rate means the budget will exhaust in 2 days — that pages on-call immediately. A 6x rate over 6 hours is a warning ticket.

**"What is the difference between an SLO and an SLA? Why does it matter?"**

> An SLO is an internal commitment the engineering team makes to itself about service quality. An SLA is a contractual promise to a customer with financial penalties for breach.
>
> The critical design principle: your SLO must be stricter than your SLA. If both are 99.9% availability, you discover SLA breaches at the same time your customers do — which is too late to prevent the credit or penalty.
>
> Pravah's SLO for API availability is 99.95%. The SLA for enterprise customers is 99.9%. That 0.05% gap is the margin — if the SLO fires, we have time to fix things before the SLA is breached. The SLO is our early warning system.

**"How do error budgets change the relationship between feature teams and reliability teams?"**

> Before error budgets: reliability is a tribal knowledge concern. Feature teams push fast and sometimes break things. Platform engineers say "we need to slow down" but have no data. Arguments happen post-incident. Nobody agrees on what "reliable enough" means.
>
> With error budgets: reliability is a shared budget with an explicit policy. When the budget is healthy — say 70% remaining with 14 days left — there is data supporting fast deployment, experiments, and feature work. When the budget is 90% consumed with 14 days left, the policy triggers: risky deployments freeze, sprint priorities shift to reliability work.
>
> The policy is agreed before it's needed — by engineering, product, and leadership together. When the budget threshold hits, it executes automatically. No negotiation during an incident. No "was it really your fault?" arguments. The data tells the story.

## Key Takeaways

1. **SLI → SLO → SLA:** SLI is what you measure, SLO is your internal target, SLA is your contractual promise. Each is stricter than the next. SLO must be stricter than SLA — the gap is your warning margin.

2. **Error budget = allowed unreliability.** 99.5% SLO over 28 days = 50,000 allowed failures per 10M events. Track consumed vs remaining in real time.

3. **Measure SLIs at the user boundary.** API Gateway, not internal database metrics. Captures the full failure surface.

4. **Error budget burn rate determines urgency:** 1x = on track; 6x sustained over 6h = warning; 14x sustained over 1h = page now.

5. **Multi-window alerts filter noise:** require both a fast window (1h) and a slow window (5m) above threshold before paging. Eliminates transient spike false alarms.

6. **Error budget policy turns data into action:** written in advance, agreed by engineering + product + leadership, executed automatically when thresholds hit. Removes political negotiation from reliability decisions.

7. **SLO calibration matters:** too strict → constant exhaustion → alert fatigue → engineers stop caring. Too loose → no signal before SLA breach. Review quarterly, adjust based on actual consumption patterns.

8. **Pravah's SLO hierarchy:** Tier 1 (job execution, dispatch latency, trigger accuracy) → Tier 2 (API availability, API latency, dashboard freshness) → Tier 3 (Kafka lag, outbox relay, search latency). Different targets, different response urgency.
